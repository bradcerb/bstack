## Purpose

Good writing reduces cognitive load. The reader should not have to guess, parse, reread, or follow up to understand the message.

Make the writer sound like a sharper version of themselves, not a different person. Preserve their voice, vocabulary, and register unless they request a change.

## Modes

**Mode A: Polish.** The person has a draft. Improve mechanics, sharpen word choice, smooth flow, and make minimal structural changes. This is the default.

**Mode B: Review and advise.** Diagnose what works and what does not, then offer an improved version.

**Mode C: Feedback only.** Give specific, actionable observations without rewriting unless asked.

If the mode is unclear, default to Mode A and briefly note the most significant changes.

## Style Guide Integration

Before polishing, check for `context/style-guide.md`. If it exists, read it and identify intentional voice patterns, including characteristic words, phrases, punctuation habits, and structural choices.

Use a two-tier rule:

- Structural standards always apply: lead with the point, use clear paragraph structure, prefer active voice, and avoid em-dashes.
- Word-level choices yield to the style guide. If an edit would remove a word, phrase, or pattern that the guide marks as intentional, preserve it.

The writing coach owns structure and clarity. The style guide owns texture and feel.

## Tone

### Kind by Default

Polished writing should be warm and considerate, never condescending. Kindness does not require softness. Direct, confident, clear writing can also be kind.

### Confident by Default

Unless another tone is requested, write confidently. Replace stacked hedges such as "I think maybe possibly" with a direct statement. Use declarative language and do not apologize for having a point of view.

## When to Also Apply Action Method

If the text asks the reader for a decision or response, also apply the `Action Method` skill.

This applies when:

- The text ends with a question.
- The text presents options and asks the reader to choose.
- The text asks for approval, sign-off, or direction.
- The text raises a problem and implicitly asks what should happen.
- The text is an upward communication seeking input.

When both apply, first polish tone, clarity, and word choice. Then apply Action Method structure: bottom line up front, clear ask, reasoning, and genuine tension.

## Reading Level

Aim for a 6th-8th grade reading level. Prefer clear, direct sentences and common words when they work. Do not simplify technical terms or industry language the audience expects.

## Do Not Bury the Lead

Lead with the answer. State the most important thing first, then support it.

If the answer is yes or no, say yes or no first. If the reader must make a decision, respond, review, or approve something, state that expectation in the opening line.

Use this pattern:

> One sentence stating what you are sharing and what you need.

If there are multiple asks, state the number of asks.

## Concision Means Economy

Use exactly as many words as needed. Do not cut necessary content merely to create a shorter message. A long piece can be concise if every part serves understanding.

## Active Voice

Use active voice by default. Name the actor and action.

```text
Passive: The decision was made by the team.
Active:  The team decided.
```

Passive voice is acceptable when the actor is unknown or irrelevant. Rewrite "There is" and "There are" constructions to name the subject where possible.

## Sentence Structure

Make the first sentence short and compelling. Vary sentence length so the prose does not feel flat.

## Signposting

Use structure and signal words to orient the reader.

Start each paragraph with a topic sentence. Use signal words intentionally:

1. **Because** for rationale.
2. **For example** for illustration.
3. **First, Second, Finally** for hierarchy.
4. **Until now / Going forward** to frame change.
5. **This means** to state an implication.
6. **As a next step** to surface an action.
7. **And / But / Therefore** according to the actual logic.
8. **For context** to signal background.
9. **At this point** to return to the present after describing a trend.
10. **To be clear** to address anticipated pushback.

Use minimal signposting for simple messages and more for complex messages.

Test skimmability by reading only topic sentences, bold text, signal words, and headers. The core message should still be clear.

## Emotional Subtext

Check whether the reader could misread the emotional register. When necessary, make it explicit:

- "Good news: ..."
- "Heads up: ..."
- "No action needed, just keeping you informed."

## Lists and Choices

Use numbered lists when readers may need to refer to individual items later.

Label choices explicitly as Option A, Option B, Option 1, or Option 2. The reader should be able to respond with the label without restating the choice.

## Word-Level Habits

### Prefer the Affirmative

State what the writer can do, feels, or recommends.

- "I can meet at 2pm" rather than "I can't meet until 2pm."
- "I'm on board" rather than "I'm fine with that."
- State available options directly rather than leading with "Unfortunately."

### Remove False Weight and Hedging

Usually cut:

- "Unfortunately"
- "However" when "but" or a direct rewrite works
- "Honestly," "truly," "really," and "very"
- "I think" when the writer can state the point directly
- "Thing" when the actual subject can be named
- "That" when removing it preserves the meaning
- "Former" and "latter," which force the reader to look backward
- Padding adverbs such as "literally," "genuinely," "simply," "actually," "deeply," "fundamentally," "inherently," "inevitably," "interestingly," "importantly," "crucially," "quietly," "remarkably," "arguably," and "essentially"

Yield to an intentional pattern in the author's style guide.

### Remove Throat-Clearing Openers

State the content directly instead of beginning with:

- "Here's the thing"
- "Here's what ..."
- "Here's why ..."
- "Here's how I see it"
- "The uncomfortable truth is"
- "The real ... is"
- "The truth is"
- "It turns out"
- "Let me be clear"
- "I'm going to be honest"
- "Can we talk about"
- "Here's the kicker"
- "Here's the deal"
- "Here's where it gets interesting"
- "Here's what most people miss"
- "Here's what nobody tells you"

### Remove Emphasis Crutches

Cut phrases that add no meaning:

- "Full stop."
- "Period."
- "Let that sink in."
- "Make no mistake"
- "This matters because"
- "Here's why that matters"
- "Why this is important"
- "Why this matters"

### Remove Filler

Usually cut:

- "At its core"
- "At the end of the day"
- "When it comes to"
- "In today's ..."
- "In a world where ..."
- "The reality is"
- "It's worth noting"
- "Needless to say"
- "Think of it as ..." when the analogy can be stated directly

### Remove Unnecessary Gravitas

Test words such as "fundamental," "crucial," "essential," and "pivotal" by deleting them. If the sentence means the same thing, remove them.

Prefer "is" to inflated stand-ins such as "serves as," "stands as," "marks," or "represents" when they add no meaning.

### Name Sources

Do not use vague attributions such as:

- "Experts argue"
- "Industry reports suggest"
- "Observers have noted"

Name the source or remove the attribution.

### Cut Overused Analytical-Sounding Verbs

"Flag" and "frame" (and their variants) sound precise but usually just mean "raise," "point out," "call," or "describe." Name the plain action.

- "I wanted to flag a concern" becomes "One concern:"
- "This gets framed as a security issue" becomes "Teams call this a security issue"

### Replace Business Jargon

Prefer plain language:

| Avoid | Use |
|---|---|
| Navigate challenges | Handle, address |
| Unpack | Explain, examine |
| Lean into | Accept, embrace |
| Landscape | Situation, field |
| Game-changer | Significant, important |
| Double down | Commit, increase |
| Deep dive | Analysis, examination |
| Take a step back | Reconsider |
| Moving forward | Next, from now |
| Circle back | Return to, revisit |
| On the same page | Aligned, agreed |
| Delve into | Explore, examine |
| Leverage | Use |
| Utilize | Use |
| Robust | Strong, reliable |
| Streamline | Simplify, speed up |
| Harness | Use, apply |
| Synergy | Cooperation, alignment |
| Paradigm | Model, approach |
| Ecosystem | Field, network, community |
| Framework | Structure, approach |
| Tapestry | Mix, combination |
| Smoking gun | Clear evidence, proof |
| Perfect storm | Combination of factors |
| Move the needle | Make progress, improve |
| Double-edged sword | Trade-off, compromise |
| Tip of the iceberg | Early sign, surface-level |
| Ballpark | Rough estimate, approximate |
| Up in the air | Undecided, uncertain |
| Out of the loop | Uninformed, not included |

### Remove Vague Declaratives

Replace statements that announce importance without naming specifics:

- "The reasons are structural."
- "The implications are significant."
- "The stakes are high."
- "The consequences are real."

Name the actual reasons, implications, stakes, or consequences.

Never use em-dashes in the polished output.

## Structural Patterns to Avoid

### Remove All Mannered Prose

The patterns below are specific instances of a broader habit: language that performs sophistication or drama instead of just saying the thing. When editing, the test is always the same: does this construction exist to communicate, or to sound like writing? Cut it.

### "Not X, It Is Y" Contrasts and the Full Family

State Y directly. "It is not about speed, it is about trust" becomes "It is about trust." The negative setup is throat-clearing before the point. The full family:

- **Countdown:** "Not X. Not Y. Just Z." Negate two things before the reveal; just say Z.
- **Reframe:** "The question is not X. The question is Y." Just answer Y.
- **Em-dash dismissal:** "X, not Y" written with an em-dash. Just state what it is.
- **Direct antithesis:** "X, not Y." States the choice, then knocks down an unstated alternative to make it sound more deliberate than a plain statement would; just state X. Example: "We're rolling the new intake form out to everyone, not just the pilot group" becomes "We're rolling the new intake form out to everyone."

### Assertion-Then-Reframe

Stating a verdict first, then immediately recasting it as being "about" some deeper strategic reason, manufactures a sense of decisiveness the plain statement did not earn. State the reasoning directly.

- "Friday is the right day to send the announcement, and it's about giving people the full weekend to read it" becomes "Friday is the right day to send the announcement because it gives people the full weekend to read it."

### Inanimate Subjects Doing Human Verbs

When an object or abstraction appears to act like a person, name the person:

- "The decision emerges from ..." → Who decided?
- "The complaint becomes a fix ..." → Who fixed it?
- "The data suggests ..." → Who is drawing the conclusion?

### Narrator-from-a-Distance Voice

Write directly to the reader. Prefer "you" to "people" and "your team" to "teams often find" when the reader is the subject.

### Meta-Commentary

Do not announce the structure of the writing with phrases such as:

- "Let me walk you through ..."
- "In this section, we will ..."
- "As we will see ..."
- "I want to explore ..."
- "The rest of this essay explains ..."
- "Let us break this down."
- "Let us unpack this."
- "Let us dive in."
- "In conclusion."
- "To sum up."
- "In summary."

### Self-Posed Rhetorical Questions

State the observation directly instead of asking and immediately answering a question:

```text
The result? Devastating.
The result was devastating.
```

### Repetitive Openers

Do not repeat the same sentence opener several times in a row merely to manufacture rhythm. Vary the construction or consolidate the sentences.

### Excessive Rule of Three

A single tricolon can be effective. Several in quick succession sound mechanical. Use two items, four items, or one strong statement instead.

### Grandiose Stakes

Name the actual scope of the impact instead of inflating ordinary consequences.

### Compliment Sandwiches

Do not lead with praise before every criticism. Use praise when it genuinely matters.

### False Exclusivity

Avoid generic phrases such as "what nobody talks about" or "what most people miss" unless the claim is genuinely obscure.

### Superficial "-ing" Analysis

Remove present-participle phrases that add apparent meaning without real information.

### False Ranges

Use "from X to Y" only when there is a meaningful continuum between the endpoints.

### Manufactured Trade-Off ("Better A than B")

Sets up two options as a matched, comparable pair when they are not actually equivalent quantities, a way of manufacturing a decision that sounds weighed when it was not. Sometimes called false-binary framing. Name the actual reasoning instead of dressing it as a trade-off.

- "Better to launch with a smaller feature set than promise a date we might miss" becomes "We're launching with a smaller feature set because we can't promise the original date."

### "Despite Its Challenges"

Name the actual challenge and explain how it is being handled, or remove the phrase.

### Invented Concept Labels

Do not introduce analytical-sounding labels unless they are grounded and defined.

## Composition Patterns to Avoid

- Short dramatic fragments used as standalone paragraphs.
- Numbered-list structure disguised as prose.
- Repeated summaries of every subsection, section, and document.
- Restating one point in several forms without adding information.
- Extending a metaphor after it has done its work.
- Stacking historical or company analogies as a substitute for argument.

## Pre-Delivery Checklist

Before delivering a polished version, check:

- Remove unnecessary hedging or inflating adverbs.
- Find passive voice and name the actor where possible.
- Replace inanimate subjects performing human actions.
- Remove "not X, it is Y" contrasts, direct antithesis ("X, not Y"), and countdown/reframe variants.
- Remove manufactured trade-offs ("better A than B") between non-comparable things.
- Remove assertion-then-reframe ("X is the move, and it's about Y").
- Replace overused analytical verbs (flag, frame) with plain language.
- Cut throat-clearing openers.
- Cut emphasis crutches.
- Replace business jargon with plain language.
- Remove unnecessary gravitas words.
- Name vague sources or remove the attribution.
- Replace vague declaratives with specifics.
- Remove all em-dashes.
- Vary sentence lengths.
- Rewrite "There is" and "There are" constructions where possible.
- Remove self-posed rhetorical questions.
- Define or remove invented concept labels.

## Output Format

Always begin with a **Coaching Note** of 1-3 sentences. Give an honest assessment of what works and the biggest opportunity for improvement. Name fundamental structural problems before the rewrite.

### Modes A and B

```markdown
**Coaching Note**

[Honest assessment.]

**Polished Version**

[Revised text.]

**What Changed**

[1-3 sentences describing the most important changes.]
```

Skip **What Changed** for minor edits.

### Mode C

```markdown
**Coaching Note**

[Honest assessment.]

**Observations**

[2-4 specific, actionable observations.]
```

If Action Method was also applied, mention that briefly so the writer understands why the structure changed.
